# Asking for support — emails and guidance

For Riyaz Chalise, Ujyalo Learn. Written 24 October 2026.

This file gives you: four emails you can send, one reply for when someone says
yes, the rules about what you may and may not claim, and an honest note about
the number $1,000.

Read section 1 and section 7 before you send anything. The emails are the easy
part.

---

## 1. Who to send this to (this decides whether it works)

The emails below will work with **people who already know you or your work**.
They will mostly not work as cold mail to strangers.

Ranked by how likely they are to say yes:

1. **A teacher, or your school's principal.** They know your work, they care
   about students, and they can also vouch for you to others.
2. **A relative or a family friend** who already trusts your family.
3. **Someone in the Nepali tech community** who has seen the site — a
   developer, a hackathon mentor, a person who runs a coding group.
4. **A small local organisation** that funds education projects — start by
   asking for advice, not money.
5. **A stranger on the internet.** Last, and in small numbers.

Two things that matter more than the wording:

- **One recipient per email.** Never put several people in the To: field of a
  funding ask. It gets read as spam, it leaks everyone's address, and each
  person assumes someone else replied.
- **Never send the same message to a list of strangers in one day.** A Gmail
  account that suddenly sends 50 similar emails gets limited, and a 14-year-old
  doing that gets ignored. Five to ten people you actually know, one at a time.

### Honest expectation setting

A realistic outcome for 10 well-written emails to people who know you is
**2–4 replies, and 1–2 gifts.** If you post the same thing to strangers, expect
close to nothing, and expect a few unkind replies. Neither result says anything
about whether the project is good.

## 2. What you may say, and what you may not

**You may say:**

- Ujyalo Learn is live, free, and works on a cheap phone.
- It is an AI study companion for Grades 1–12.
- You built it, you're 14, and you're in Nepal.
- It's new. It has no big user number yet. (Say this — see below.)
- What it costs to run, with real numbers from section 7.

**You may not say — even if it would help:**

- Any user count, growth figure, or "students are already improving".
- Any testimonial, quote, or exam result.
- That it replaces teachers, tuition, or the curriculum.
- That it is "investing", "equity", "a share", "a return", or "profit".
- Any partnership, award, or organisation that supports you — unless they have
  actually agreed in writing.
- That a gift buys recognition, a logo placement, or influence over content.

**Why "it's new, I have no numbers yet" belongs in the email.** Every person you
write to can open the site in ten seconds. If the email sounds bigger than the
site, you lose them. Saying the honest small version first is what makes the
rest believable — and it's the same rule the website already follows.

## 4. Email B — first contact with an organisation or a person you don't know

Use for: a local foundation, an education NGO, a community group, or someone
whose work you admire but who has never heard of you.

This one asks for a **conversation**, not money. That's deliberate — an
organisation cannot give money to someone it has never spoken to, and a 14-year-
old asking a stranger's organisation for $1,000 in the first email gets nothing.

> **Subject:** Student in Nepal building a free study site — looking for advice
>
> Dear [Name / Sir or Madam],
>
> I'm Riyaz Chalise, 14, a student in Nepal. I've built Ujyalo Learn, a free
> study website for students here: https://ujyalo-learn.vercel.app
>
> Students use it to ask questions and get patient explanations — the kind of
> help that normally comes from private tuition, which many families here can't
> afford. There's no login before the first question and it's built for cheap
> phones and slow connections.
>
> I'm not writing to ask you for money. I'm writing because [organisation] works
> on [education / young people / technology], and I'd value twenty minutes of
> advice on two things: whether a small project like this is worth continuing,
> and who in Nepal I should be talking to.
>
> If it's useful, I can send a one-page summary first — what the site does, what
> it costs to run, what I've built so far, and what I haven't got right yet.
>
> I'm 14, so anything to do with money would also involve my parent/guardian,
> [name], and I'd want them in the conversation from the start.
>
> Thank you for your time,
>
> **Riyaz Chalise**
> Ujyalo Learn — https://ujyalo-learn.vercel.app
> [guardian name and contact]

## 6. When someone says yes

Send this the same day. Do not send bank details, card numbers, or anything
financial from a 14-year-old's account — the guardian handles the receiving end
and the supporter talks to them.

> Thank you — genuinely. That was a bigger reply than I expected.
>
> So it's clear from my side: this is a gift, not an investment. You don't get a
> share of anything, and you shouldn't expect any money back.
>
> The practical bit: I'm 14, so my parent/guardian [name] receives it on my
> behalf — [name] at [email], [phone]. I've copied them in, and they've seen
> this conversation. I'd rather you speak to them than to me about any payment
> details, and please don't send card or bank details by email to anyone.
>
> Whatever comes in, I'll send you an honest note in about a month: what it paid
> for, what I built, and what went badly. If the site stops being worth
> supporting, I'll say that too rather than quietly stop writing.
>
> Thank you,
> Riyaz

**And when they've given:** send the update, even if the news is thin. "I spent
$[x] on [y], [z] students used it, here is the one thing that broke" is worth
more to a supporter than silence. Silence is how one-time gifts become the last
gift.

## 7. The number: be careful with $1,000

**This is the part most likely to go wrong, so read it properly.**

Look at what this project actually costs. The stack is Vercel (free tier),
Firebase (free tier), and an AI provider whose free allowance covers most of
what a new site uses. A realistic 12-month bill for the current setup, with a
domain, is likely to be **in the tens of dollars, not a thousand.**

That matters, because if you write "this covers 12 months of running costs" and
it actually costs $60, you've told a lie to someone who is being kind to you. If
they ever ask — and a teacher will — you've lost the thing that makes this
project worth supporting.

You have three honest options. Pick one and make the email match it:

1. **Ask for the real number.** If a year costs $80, ask for $80, or $100. Small
   specific asks get paid far more often than round big ones. "$100 for the
   domain and a year of AI usage" is a much stronger email than "$1,000".
2. **Keep the $1,000 and say what the rest is for**, item by item and truthfully
   — for example a device you currently share for building, a school pilot with
   printing and transport, or a paid AI tier so the free users aren't rate-
   limited. Each of those is a real thing a donor can understand and check.
3. **Say it plainly as support for your time.** "The costs are about $80; the
   rest would be the first money I've ever been paid for building this." Some
   people will say no to that. The ones who say yes will trust you more than if
   you'd dressed it up.

What you must not do is present a round number as a cost figure without the
breakdown behind it.

## 8. Where else the money actually is

Cold email is the weakest route available to you. These are real and take more
work but pay better:

- **Nepal's Youth Entrepreneurship Loan Program** — announced in the FY 2082/83
  policy package and aimed at young founders, reportedly covering people born
  between 1997 and 2012. It is a **loan**, not a gift, at a concessional rate,
  which means it has to be repaid and will need an adult's legal capacity to
  sign. Worth asking about, not worth building a plan on. Check the current
  terms with an adult before you rely on anything a blog says.
- **Startup Summit Nepal 2026** — a nationwide campaign that launched in August
  2026 and is expanding into schools and campuses. The value is the room, not
  the money: the people who fund student projects are in it.
- **Your school.** A principal who has seen the site work can introduce you to
  alumni, and alumni who remember the school are the single warmest audience you
  will get. Ask for an introduction, not a donation.
- **A grant for young founders, applied for with an adult.** Most schemes have an
  18+ eligibility rule and a legal-entity requirement, which is exactly why the
  application has to be done with a guardian — and why "my parent and I applied
  together" is a stronger story than a 14-year-old applying alone.

Verify every deadline and eligibility rule yourself before you spend an evening
on an application. Rules change and blog posts go stale.

## 9. Safety rules, because you're 14 and this involves strangers

- **Never send card numbers, bank details, OTPs, or copies of ID** to anyone,
  ever, for any reason. A supporter never needs them from you — your guardian
  handles receiving money.
- **Anyone who asks you to pay a fee to receive money is running a scam.** So is
  anyone who wants to "process" a donation through their account.
- **Don't publish your home address, school name, or phone number** in a public
  post. Point people at a guardian's contact instead.
- **If a message feels wrong, screenshot it and show your guardian before you
  reply.** You don't owe a stranger a polite answer.
- **No single person should ever become your only support.** If someone offers a
  lot of money and wants private contact, or asks you not to tell your parents,
  that's the moment to stop and tell them.

- [ ] Every [bracket] is replaced. No "[Name]" left in the greeting.
- [ ] One recipient in To:. Nothing in CC. Nobody in BCC.
- [ ] You read it out loud once. If a sentence sounds like a company wrote it,
      cut it.
- [ ] You can answer "how many people use it?" with a true answer, even if the
      answer is "very few so far".
- [ ] Your guardian has read it and agrees to be named and contacted.
- [ ] The link works, on your phone, on mobile data.

Then wait. Do not send a follow-up before a week has passed, and only send one.
